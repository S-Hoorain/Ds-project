ASSIGNMENT 2
Predicting Early Hospital Readmission (Diabetes Dataset)
================================================================================


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. OVERVIEW & ASSIGNMENT PURPOSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When hospital patients with chronic conditions like diabetes are discharged,
some end up returning to the hospital shortly afterward. Predicting which
patients are at higher risk of returning allows healthcare teams to provide
extra support and follow-up care before the patient leaves.

The aim of this assignment is to build machine learning models to identify
patients who might need additional attention after discharge, using a
hospitalization dataset. There is no single "correct" modeling recipe. You will
explore the data, make thoughtful decisions about cleaning and splitting it,
test different algorithms, and explain why your approach makes sense.

Note that the submission for the assignment is highly standardized for
evaluation purposes.

  What is standardized:  the prediction target, the training data, and the
                         submission format (Sections 2, 3 and 5).
  What is open:          how you build and pre-process your model.

This will allow evaluation of your submitted model against a set of hidden
patients.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE PREDICTION TASK (COMMON TO ALL TEAMS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All teams must predict early readmission:

  Positive class (1):  readmitted == "<30"
                       (patient returned within 30 days of discharge)

  Negative class (0):  readmitted is ">30" or "NO"

Your model must output, for each hospital stay (row), the predicted probability
that the stay is followed by an early readmission.

You are free to explore other framings (any readmission, three-class
prediction) as an optional extension in your report, but your submitted model
is evaluated ONLY on the binary early-readmission task stated above.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. OPEN-ENDED DIRECTIONS TO EXPLORE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Decide as a team how you will address each area:

4.1 Defining Your Prediction Target
    The target is fixed (Section 2). In your report, explain why early
    readmission is a practical framing for hospital staff, and discuss any
    limitations (e.g., class imbalance, what a positive prediction should
    trigger).

4.2 Cleaning & Grouping Features
    Look closely at the raw values. Notice how missing information is recorded
    (such as "?") and how columns like diagnoses contain hundreds of specific
    codes. Explore ways to simplify, clean, or combine these features into
    broader categories. Remember that all cleaning must be reproducible on new
    raw data (Section 5).

4.3 Thoughtful Data Splitting & Validation
    Some patients appear in multiple rows (patient_nbr). If the same patient
    appears in both training and validation data, your results will be
    unrealistically optimistic. Consider your options for sorting this out.

4.4 Exploring Machine Learning Algorithms
    Do not rely on a single model. Compare simple baselines with more flexible
    algorithms, and discuss the trade-offs between simplicity,
    interpretability, and predictive performance.

4.5 Evaluating Errors & Decision Cutoffs
    Failing to spot a high-risk patient is not the same as giving extra
    follow-up to someone who turns out fine. Experiment with decision
    thresholds beyond 50%, and justify the single threshold you submit (see
    Section 5). Choose it using your development data only.

4.6 Checking Performance Across Patient Groups
    Investigate whether your model performs equally well across age groups and
    other demographic categories (e.g., gender, race). Report subgroup sample
    sizes alongside performance, and discuss which groups show higher error
    rates.

------------------------

Your preprocessing and model must be bundled so that predictions can be made
directly from raw, unlabeled rows, in the same format as diabetes_dev.csv but
without the "readmitted" column.

All cleaning, grouping, encoding, and imputation must happen inside your
predict code or your saved pipeline. Do not rely on state left in your notebook
or markdown file.

command line shown.

    if __name__ == "__main__":

5.3 Rules Your Code Must Follow
-------------------------------

  1. Load the model from the model/ folder using relative paths only (no
     absolute paths such as C:\Users\...). Save Python pipelines with joblib
     (a scikit-learn Pipeline, including preprocessing) and R models with
     saveRDS.

  2. Never retrain inside predict code. Prediction must be deterministic, and
     must not need internet access or the training data.

  3. Do not use encounter_id or patient_nbr as predictive features. Held-out
     patients are unseen, so these cannot help and may break your code.

  4. Handle unseen categories, "?" values, and missing columns' values
     gracefully. The held-out data may contain category levels (e.g., rare
     diagnosis codes) not present in the development data. Your code must not
     crash on them.

  5. Preserve row count and order: one probability per input row, in [0, 1],
     with no NaN values.

  6. Use only widely available libraries:
       Python: numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, xgboost, lightgbm, scipy, joblib
       R:      standard CRAN packages
     Pin versions in requirements.txt (Python) or report sessionInfo() in
     env.txt (R). Record your language version in README.md.

  7. Runtime: predictions for ~20,000 rows must finish in under 5 minutes on an
     ordinary laptop (no GPU).
